# Where the Pretend Internet came from

*A public-facing account of the idea, not a transcript or a claim that every experiment worked.*

## Why “pretend”?

Pretend does not mean fake or worthless. It describes the freedom to build a small version of something before the outside world has a place for it—and to make imagined places tangible enough to visit, use, share, and return to.

The project has a personal origin. Melody brought an unusually direct, playful perspective to work that had become buried in technical abstractions. A recovered catalog needed pictures, recognizable categories, clear working/broken/unknown signals, and a way for a person to correct a computer's confident mistakes. Those ideas helped turn the catalog into something more approachable.

Later, development stalled on the absence of a public host. Melody's response was to make a little server and a *pretend Internet*: a smaller, local place where real behavior could be exercised without waiting for the final infrastructure. That was not a proposal to fake a successful deployment. It was a way to ask a smaller, testable question.

The human reason matters as much as the engineering: ideas, people, and places do not become valueless because they are not immediately visible or recognized. The project explores how an imagined thing can be given an accessible form. Its worth is not conditional on becoming commercially useful.

## A brief, source-recovered chronology

- **September 23–24, 2026 — HERE YOU GO and the little network.** A pre-existing technical catalog was presented as a pretend app store; Melody contributed a much more human interface. A separate hosting blocker prompted the local baby-server/pretend-Internet suggestion. The preserved conversation reports follow-on prototypes and testing plans, but does not independently establish every runtime result.
- **Late September–early October — linked little sites.** Nostr, nsites, portable content, local databases, addresses and lightweight sites became experimental building materials. The aim was exploration, not a predetermined universal stack.
- **Early October — a room inside the computer.** Portable workspaces, personal places, persistence and playful interfaces became visible directions. Melody's room and the larger workbench are related but not interchangeable.
- **October — from catalog to actual experiences.** A catalog of discovered artifacts was distinguished from a store or collection of genuinely runnable applications. Prototypes could not stand in for the experiences they promised.
- **October–November — Studio and the portfolio.** Work shifted toward restoring, building, maintaining, packaging and connecting independent experiences. The later scope clarification establishes a portfolio, **not** a compulsory monolithic launcher, Nostr stack or shared runtime.

The underlying catalog, source recovery and other Nostr/software experiments predated Melody's specific interventions. This account credits her contribution without assigning all the preceding engineering to one moment.

## From an idea to something usable

The dream is a small, browsable world of real places: pages, programs, games, music, books, tools and archives, owned and connected in ways that ordinary people can understand.

The working test is modest: **Find → Open → Use → Keep → Return**. A real piece of the world should pass those steps on its intended device before it is described as shipped.
